**Peter Pan; or, The Boy Who Could Not Keep Anything**

---

All children grow up, except one, and the one who does not pays for it in a currency he cannot count.

This is the part of the story that is usually left out, so we shall put it in first, and then get on to the flying, which is the part everyone wants.

---

**I. The Nursery**

Mrs. Darling first heard of Peter when she was tidying her children's minds, which is what mothers do at night — going through the day's contents and putting the nicer thoughts on top where they will be found first in the morning. In Wendy's mind, again and again, in a child's handwriting that was not quite Wendy's, was the name *Peter*.

"Who is Peter?" she asked.

"He comes in the window," said Wendy, "and he doesn't stay."

The second half of that sentence was the true half, and Wendy was the only person in the house who had noticed it.

He came for his shadow. It had come off, as shadows will when a boy moves faster than a shadow can agree to, and Nana had shut it in the drawer, and Peter had come back for it in a temper. Wendy found him sitting on the nursery floor at midnight, sobbing furiously, trying to stick it on with soap.

"That isn't how," said Wendy. "It wants sewing."

"I knew that," said Peter, who had not.

She sewed it on. It hurt him and he did not say so, which she thought was rather fine of him, and he crowed when it took, which she thought was rather much. Then he looked at her with his head on one side, the way a bird looks at a thing it has not decided about, and said:

"Have I been here before?"

"You have," said Wendy. "Mother says you came when I was small. You left footprints on the sill."

"Did I?" said Peter, and he was so entirely delighted, so completely without any weight of having-already-done-it, that Wendy felt something go cold in her that she had no name for yet. It would be years before she found the name. The name was *pity*, and it is a terrible thing to feel for the first time at twelve, at midnight, for a boy who is laughing.

---

**II. The Flight**

Now the joy, because the joy is real, and anyone who tells you the joy is not real has not understood the trouble.

They flew.

Wendy would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it and she never stopped trying. It was not like swimming and it was not like falling and it was not like the dreams in which you fly, which are always a little anxious, a little effortful, the dreamer suspecting the floor. It was like being *agreed with*. The whole night moved over to make room.

Michael flew badly and joyfully. John flew carefully and kept his hat on. Wendy flew as if she had been waiting to and had only just been told the waiting was over.

And Peter — Peter flew the way other people breathe, which is to say without any sense that it was an achievement, and this was the first hint of the whole trouble. He could not be pleased about it. He had nothing to compare it to. He had never once in his life not been able to fly, and so he had never once been *given* it.

He showed off dreadfully. He flew backwards. He shouted. He forgot the others were behind him and had to be called, twice.

The second time, Wendy called, "Peter!" and he turned round in the air with a perfectly pleasant, perfectly empty face, and said, "Hullo — who are you?"

And then a heartbeat later, "*Wendy!*" — with such enormous warmth, such genuine gladness at the sight of her, that she almost let it go.

Almost.

---

**III. The Island**

Neverland is different for every child, but it always has the same architecture: something to be afraid of, something to eat, and something to be the hero of. Wendy's had mermaids in it. John's had a wrecked ship. Michael's had a great many caves and one enormous friendly beast that never quite appeared.

The Lost Boys were called Tootles, Nibs, Slightly, Curly, and the Twins, and they had a hole each in the ground under a tree, and no mothers, and a great many opinions about mothers. They accepted Wendy as one instantly and totally, which is the way children accept things and the way adults have entirely lost.

She told them stories in the evenings. She darned. She made them take medicine that was really water and she said it was medicine and they believed her because they wanted the ceremony more than the cure, and this is the truest thing about children that anyone ever wrote down.

And the games were wonderful and the days were long and warm and blurred, and Peter was the best of all of them at everything, and it was very nearly perfect.

Only —

Only, one evening, Slightly said, "Tell about the time Peter fought the wolves," and the boys all leaned in, and Peter leaned in too, with his chin on his fists, entirely rapt.

He listened to his own adventure like a boy at a bedtime story.

At the good part he shouted, "Was he really? Was he *really*?" and Nibs said, "It was *you*, Peter," and Peter said, "I know it was me," in a voice that meant he had not known, and would not know again by morning, and the boys laughed because it was funny, and it *was* funny, and Wendy laughed too and then went and stood by the entrance for a moment looking at nothing.

---

**IV. Hook**

Now we must speak of Captain Jas. Hook, and we must be careful, because he was not what the story says he was. The story says he was a villain. He was, but that was the least of him.

Hook remembered everything.

That was his affliction and his entire character. He remembered his school and its motto and the shape of the light through the window in the second-form room. He remembered every insult, in order, with dates. He remembered his mother. He remembered the exact sound the clock made in the crocodile's belly the first time he heard it and he had heard it every day since and it had never once become ordinary.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Every man who remembers is being followed by something with a clock in it. Hook simply had the honesty to be followed by his out loud.

And this is why he hated Peter — not for the hand. The hand was nothing; he had learned to write with the other one and his handwriting was, if anything, improved. He hated Peter because Peter had taken the hand and *lost the memory of taking it*. Hook had given up a piece of his body to a boy who could not keep it. All that pain, and nobody at the other end holding on to it.

"Do you know me?" Hook would call across the water, every time, always the same words.

"You're a codfish!" Peter would call back, delighted, because a codfish was funny.

"You cut off my hand."

"Did I?" — and here Peter's face would do the thing that made Hook want to put his own eyes out — the face would fill up with *pride*, brand-new pride, pride at hearing a wonderful story about a boy who sounded splendid. "*Did I really?*"

That was the worst hour of Hook's life, and he lived it about once a fortnight.

---

**V. The Fight**

The end of Hook came as such things do: on a deck, at night, with the boys cheering.

They fought well. Hook fought better than Peter, which nobody ever mentions, being the more practiced and much the stronger, and he was losing anyway, because Peter fought without any of the small hesitations that come from knowing what a wound is like.

At the last, when it was clear, Hook stopped.

He lowered his blade — good form, always, the one thing he had left — and he looked at the boy in front of him with an expression his men had never seen and could not have named.

"You won't remember this," he said.

"I'll remember," said Peter.

"You will not remember my name by the winter," said Hook, quite gently, and it was the only gentle thing he ever said. "I have thought about you every day for thirty years. You have not thought about me since Tuesday. Do you understand what you are? You are a boy that nothing sticks to. You are having a marvellous time, and there is nobody in there having it."

Peter did not understand a word of it, and so — and this is the part Wendy could not forget, could not put down, carried into her forties — Peter *laughed*. Warmly. Fondly. The way you laugh at a friend being silly.

Then he did what he did, and Hook went into the water, and the clock was waiting, and it was still ticking, and Hook heard it, and Hook did not mind as much as everyone assumed.

Being remembered by something is not the worst way to go.

---

**VI. The Light**

Tinker Bell drank the poison meant for Peter. She did it quickly, before he noticed, in the manner of small fierce things, and her light went low and yellow and then nearly out.

You know this part. She could be saved if children believed in fairies, and Peter cried out to everyone who might be dreaming anywhere, *clap if you believe*, and the clapping came from everywhere at once, and she came back, blazing, furious, alive.

It is a lovely scene and it is quite right that it is famous.

Here is the part that is not famous.

Some seasons later — not many — Wendy asked after her. Just conversationally. Just: *how is Tinker Bell?*

And Peter said, "Who?"

And Wendy said, "*Tink.* Your — Tinker Bell. She nearly died for you. You called out to the whole world for her."

And Peter said, with the easy kindness that was the cruellest thing about him, "Fairies don't live long, you know. They're so small there isn't room in them for much." He said it as a general fact, the way you'd remark on the weather. "I expect there was one, though. There usually is."

*I expect there was one.*

He was not being cold. That is the thing to understand, if you understand nothing else about him. He was not cold at all. He was the warmest creature Wendy ever met. He simply had a bucket with a hole in it, and he had never once seen the bucket full, and so he did not know that anything was leaking.

---

**VII. Going Home**

They went home because Wendy remembered her mother, and remembering is the beginning of growing up — that is what growing up *is*, it is only remembering, day after day, until the days have a shape.

Peter came to the window and would not come in.

Mrs. Darling would have kept him. She said so; she meant it; she had a bed ready in her mind before she had one ready in the house. He would have had to go to school, she explained, and then to an office, and be a man.

"I don't want to be a man," said Peter, and his voice went up very high. "I want always to be a little boy and to have fun."

It was the only moment in his whole existence when he seemed to know what he was refusing. It lasted about two seconds. Then it was gone, and he was laughing again, and he flew off, and he was — this must be said plainly — he was perfectly happy. He remained perfectly happy. He has been perfectly happy this entire time.

That is not a consolation. Read it again. It is the opposite of one.

---

**VIII. The Coda**

He came back for the spring cleaning, and forgot, and came back, and forgot, and the gaps got longer.

And one year he came back and the window was in the same place and the room was the same room, and Wendy stood up out of the chair by the fire, and she was tall, and she was — there is no soft way — she was *old*, or old enough.

"Wendy!" he cried, joyful, unchanged, exactly the same boy. "Come away! There are pirates!"

She could not fly. She had not been able to for a long time. You stop being able to when you start being able to keep things; those are the same organ, and you cannot have both.

"Peter," she said. "I grew up. I grew up long ago."

He backed toward the window. He did not understand and it was making him frightened, and Peter frightened was a terrible thing, a bird against glass.

Then he steadied, and looked at her with the head-on-one-side look, and asked, quite politely, the question that broke her:

"Who is Hook?"

And Wendy sat back down in the chair and put her hands over her face, and she was not crying for herself, which is what everyone assumes. She was crying because somewhere out over the sea there was a man who had spent thirty years being furious at a boy, and the boy had gone through his whole life without once being able to hold on to him, and there was no one left to hold either of them but her, and she was one woman in a chair in Bloomsbury and she was going to die eventually and then that would be that.

She had been the memory. That was her job, from the first night, and nobody had told her she was taking it.

---

Her daughter's name was Jane, and Jane was in the little bed, and Jane was not afraid of him at all.

"Boy," said Jane, "why are you crying?"

He had not known he was. He wiped it off, puzzled, and said, "I don't know. I never cry," which was true, and which he would go on saying, and which will never stop being one of the saddest sentences in the language.

Then he took Jane to Neverland, and Wendy stood at the window and let them go, because there was nothing else to be done, and because — and she was honest enough to admit this at least — because it *is* wonderful up there, and it would have been a cruelty to keep it from her.

And when Jane grew up she had a daughter called Margaret, and Peter came for Margaret, and when Margaret grew up she had a daughter, and so it will go on, so long as children are joyful and innocent and heartless.

Only — Barrie stopped there, and I do not think that is quite the end.

The end is this: every one of those girls grows up, and every one of them, in her fifties, in a quiet house, will remember a boy she cannot see any more. And so he *is* remembered, continuously, by a chain of women handing him along one to the next, none of them able to keep him and all of them keeping him anyway.

He will never know.

That is the arrangement. It has been the arrangement all along. Somebody has to hold the thread, and it will not be the one flying.
